\section*{Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Summary}

This project turns the diabetes articles published on the website of the Vinmec hospital group into a \textbf{Knowledge Graph}: a network of small, explicit facts such as \emph{(Đái tháo đường type 2, HAS\_SYMPTOM, Khát nhiều)} or \emph{(Metformin, BELONGS\_TO\_CLASS, Biguanide)}. The facts are stored in the graph database \textbf{Neo4j}, can be queried with the Cypher language, and can be explored by anyone through a small \textbf{website} that shows every fact together with the articles it comes from.

The pipeline has four stages. A crawler reads Vinmec's sitemap, selects 1,197 candidate pages whose address mentions diabetes, downloads them politely and keeps the 1,096 that really discuss the disease (about 1.13 million words). An extractor reads each article section by section and uses the section heading (\emph{Triệu chứng}, \emph{Nguyên nhân}, \emph{Biến chứng}, \emph{Điều trị}…) to decide what kind of fact the text below it can contain; it then matches a hand-written vocabulary of 106 diabetes concepts written in 320 different ways. Each article votes for the facts it states, and a fact is kept only when at least two different articles agree. The result, loaded into Neo4j, has \textbf{127 entities of 10 types and 339 facts of 12 relation types}, linked to the 1,096 source articles.

Four results stand out:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Section headings are the key signal.} Health articles are organised under standard headings, and using them turns free Vietnamese text into typed facts without any language model. A word found in the wrong section (``mệt mỏi'' in a breakfast article) is simply ignored.
    \item \textbf{Agreement between articles removes noise.} Requiring support from at least two articles drops one-off mentions, and the support count stored on every fact lets the user choose how cautious to be: 252 facts are stated by three or more articles, 70 by ten or more.
    \item \textbf{The facts are reliable.} On a random sample of 50 extracted facts, each checked against all its source articles, \textbf{48 were correct (0.96; likely range 0.87--0.99)}. Both errors had the same cause---a fact about a complication attached to the disease itself---which led to a stricter heading rule and a clear analysis of the trade-off it brings.
    \item \textbf{The graph answers questions no single article answers.} Following two arrows---type 2 diabetes \emph{can cause} diabetic kidney disease, which \emph{affects} the kidney---answers ``which organs can type 2 damage?'', and every answer can be traced back to the articles that state each step.
\end{enumerate}

The project meets every requirement of the lecture's Mini Project 3 (at least 50 entities, 100 relationships, 5 relationship types, 3 entity types and 5 Cypher queries) and of Exercise 1 (triples stored as CSV, visualisation, one-hop and two-hop queries).

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The Application Problem}

Diabetes is one of the most common chronic diseases in Vietnam, and people who want to understand it usually read health articles on the internet. Vinmec, one of the largest private hospital groups in the country, publishes almost a thousand Vietnamese articles about diabetes alone: what its symptoms are, who is at risk, which complications it causes, how it is diagnosed, which drugs treat it, what patients should eat.

This knowledge is valuable, but it is locked inside text. A person who wants to know \emph{which complications of type 2 diabetes affect the eyes} has to find the right articles, read them, and put the pieces together themselves. The same symptom is written in different ways in different articles (\emph{uống nhiều}, \emph{khát nước}, \emph{hay khát}), and the answer to a question is often spread across several articles: one article says that type 2 diabetes can cause retinopathy, another explains that retinopathy damages the retina of the eye. A computer program has an even harder time: for it, an article is only a long sequence of characters.

The goal of this project is to turn this collection of articles into a form that a program can query directly and that a person can explore without reading hundreds of pages, while keeping the link to the original articles so that every answer can be checked.

\subsection{Why Conventional Keyword Search Is Insufficient}

The obvious tool for finding information in a collection of documents is keyword search. It fails here for three reasons.

\textbf{The same thing is written in many ways.} A search for \emph{khát nước} (thirsty) misses an article that writes \emph{uống nhiều} (drinking a lot) or \emph{khát nhiều}. A search for \emph{Metformin} misses the brand name \emph{Glucophage}. Every reader would have to know all the synonyms in advance.

\textbf{A word is not a fact.} An article about breakfast may say that eating well ``helps you feel less \emph{mệt mỏi}'' (tired). A keyword search for \emph{mệt mỏi} finds this article, but the article does not say that fatigue is a symptom of diabetes. Keyword search finds where a word appears, not what the text says about it.

\textbf{Answers that need two facts cannot be found.} No article contains the sentence ``type 2 diabetes damages the kidney'' in exactly that form; the answer comes from combining ``type 2 can cause diabetic kidney disease'' with ``diabetic kidney disease affects the kidney''. Keyword search returns documents one at a time and has no way to combine statements from different documents.

\subsection{Why a Knowledge Graph}

A Knowledge Graph stores knowledge not as sentences but as many small, separate \textbf{facts}, each connecting exactly two things with a named relationship: \emph{(subject, relation, object)}. Drawn as a picture, the things are dots (\textbf{entities} or nodes) and the facts are arrows between them (\textbf{relationships} or edges), so the whole collection forms a network---a graph.

This representation answers the three problems above directly:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Synonyms are resolved once.} When the facts are created, \emph{uống nhiều}, \emph{khát nước} and \emph{hay khát} are all mapped to one entity, \emph{Khát nhiều}. Every later question uses that single entity.
    \item \textbf{Facts are explicit and typed.} The graph only contains a fact when an article states it in a place where such a statement is made (Section~\ref{sec:extraction}), so ``fatigue is a symptom of diabetes'' is a fact only if articles really say so.
    \item \textbf{Facts can be combined by following arrows.} A question becomes a walk through the network: start at \emph{type 2}, follow \emph{can cause} to the complications, then \emph{affects} to the organs. The path itself is the explanation of the answer.
\end{itemize}

The lecture notes summarise the difference between the two kinds of retrieval: vector or keyword retrieval asks ``which passages are similar to the question?'', while graph retrieval asks ``which entities and relationships are explicitly connected?''. For questions about how symptoms, causes, complications, tests and treatments relate to each other, the second question is the right one.

A Knowledge Graph also keeps something a trained model loses: \textbf{provenance}. Every fact in this project stores the articles that state it, so any answer can be checked against its sources. For a medical topic, where a wrong answer can mislead a patient, this is essential.

\section{Problem Definition}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Input and Output}

\textbf{Input.} The public Vietnamese articles of vinmec.com that are about diabetes: Vinmec's official disease pages (short structured summaries with fixed headings: overview, causes, symptoms, risk groups, prevention, diagnosis, treatment) and the much larger set of news and advice articles written or reviewed by Vinmec doctors. No other source is used.

\textbf{Output.} A Knowledge Graph in which
\begin{itemize}
    \item every node is a clearly named diabetes concept with a type: a type of diabetes, a symptom, a risk factor, a complication, a diagnostic test, a drug, a drug class, a food, a lifestyle measure or an organ;
    \item every edge is a fact with a named relation, such as \code{HAS\_SYMPTOM} or \code{TREATED\_BY};
    \item every extracted fact records how many different articles state it (its \textbf{support}) and which articles they are;
\end{itemize}
plus three ways to use it: Cypher queries in Neo4j, a retrieval function that returns the facts around one entity, and a website.

\subsection{Questions the System Must Answer}

A Knowledge Graph is only useful if it answers real questions. The design started from the questions a reader of diabetes articles would ask, and each one corresponds to a different kind of graph query:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\caption{Questions the system must answer and the kind of graph query each one needs.}
\label{tab:questions}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{X l X}
\toprule
Question & Query type & What the graph must contain \\
\midrule
What are the symptoms of type 1 diabetes? What drugs treat type 2? & one-hop & typed facts from each diabetes type \\
What do we know about Metformin? & one-hop, both directions & facts in which Metformin is the subject or the object \\
Which organs can type 2 diabetes damage? & two-hop & complications \emph{and} the organ each complication affects \\
Which types of diabetes match these symptoms? & aggregation over one hop & symptom facts per type, with their support \\
How is obesity connected to the kidney? & shortest path & a connected graph \\
How is diabetes diagnosed, and at which values? & one-hop + node property & tests, with thresholds stored on the test nodes \\
Where does this fact come from? & provenance & article URLs stored on each fact \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

These questions decided the design. The two-hop question is the reason for the hand-added \emph{affects organ} facts (Section~\ref{sec:curated}); the symptom checker is the reason why support is stored on every fact rather than used once as a filter; the provenance question is the reason why the articles themselves are nodes in the graph.

\subsection{Success Criteria}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Size and structure} (lecture Mini Project 3 and Exercise 1): at least 50 entities, 100 relationships, 5 relationship types and 3 entity types; triples in CSV; at least five Cypher queries including one-hop and two-hop queries; a visualisation.
    \item \textbf{Correctness}: measured, not assumed. A random sample of extracted facts is checked by hand against the source articles, and the share of correct facts (precision) is reported with its uncertainty.
    \item \textbf{Traceability}: every extracted fact links to the articles that state it.
    \item \textbf{Usability}: a person without any knowledge of Cypher can find an entity, see its facts and their sources, compare diabetes types, check symptoms and find connections, through a website.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What the System Is Not}

The graph summarises what public health articles say. It is not a diagnostic tool and not medical advice. ``Correct'' in this report means that the Vinmec articles state the fact for that type of diabetes, not that a doctor has verified it against current clinical guidelines. This distinction is repeated in the website and in the evaluation, because for a medical topic it matters.

\section{Data Collection}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Why Vinmec}

Three properties made Vinmec a good single source for a Vietnamese diabetes graph. First, the language: the articles are written in Vietnamese, so the graph uses the words Vietnamese readers actually use. Second, coverage: the site has almost a thousand diabetes articles covering every aspect of the disease, from early signs to diet, drugs and pregnancy. Third, consistency: the articles are written or reviewed by the hospital's doctors and follow a recognisable structure, with headings such as \emph{Triệu chứng} (symptoms), \emph{Nguyên nhân} (causes) and \emph{Điều trị} (treatment). Section~\ref{sec:extraction} shows that this structure is what makes automatic extraction possible.

\subsection{Finding the Pages}

\paragraph{Why not the search box.} The obvious way to find diabetes articles would be the website's search box. That does not work for a program. Vinmec's search results are produced by JavaScript running inside the browser after the page has loaded. When a program downloads the search page directly, it receives the page \emph{before} the JavaScript has run, and there are no results in it. A test request for ``tiểu đường'' returned a 150~KB page containing no article links.

\paragraph{The sitemap.} Large websites publish a \textbf{sitemap}, a file listing every URL on the site, so that search engines can find all their pages. Vinmec's \code{sitemap.xml} points to about 230 smaller sitemaps. The crawler reads the ones for Vietnamese articles (\code{posts-vi-1.xml} \ldots{} \code{posts-vi-87.xml}) and Vietnamese disease pages (\code{diseases-vi-1.xml}), which together list about 222,000 URLs---the entire Vietnamese website.

\paragraph{Selecting by slug.} Every URL ends with a short readable name of the page, its \textbf{slug}, written without Vietnamese accents. The article \emph{``Bệnh đái tháo đường: chế độ ăn như thế nào?''} has the slug \code{benh-dai-thao-duong-che-do-nhu-nao-vi}. This makes it possible to select diabetes pages without downloading all 222,000. Keywords are split into two strengths because they carry different risks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{strong keywords} (\code{tieu-duong}, \code{thao-duong}, \code{dai-duong}): a slug containing one of them is almost certainly about diabetes;
    \item \textbf{weak keywords} (\code{insulin}, \code{metformin}, \code{hba1c}, \code{duong-huyet} = blood sugar, \code{glucose}, \code{sglt2}, …): the page \emph{might} be about diabetes, but these words also appear in articles about other topics, such as the role of insulin in the body or blood sugar in healthy people.
\end{itemize}
The slug \code{thao-nhat} is excluded explicitly: \emph{đái tháo nhạt} (diabetes insipidus) shares the words ``đái tháo'' with \emph{đái tháo đường} but is a different disease of the kidneys and hormones, unrelated to blood sugar.

This selection gives \textbf{1,197 candidate URLs}. Six of them either failed to download or were the same article published under a second address (the crawler skips a page whose title it has already seen), so \textbf{1,191 pages} were downloaded and checked: 944 with a strong keyword and 247 with only a weak one.

\paragraph{Politeness.} The site's \code{robots.txt} (where a website states what automated programs may access) allows these paths. The crawler identifies itself as a student research crawler, waits one second between requests and retries failed requests with increasing pauses, so it never puts noticeable load on the server. It also saves every page as soon as it is processed and skips pages already saved, so an interrupted crawl continues where it stopped.

\subsection{Why a Second Filter Is Needed}

Selecting pages by slug has a hidden problem, caused by the missing accents. Vietnamese has many words that differ only in their accents, and once the accents are removed they become identical:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \code{tieu-duong} is \emph{tiểu đường} (diabetes), but also the beginning of \emph{tiểu dương vật} (a urology question about a child);
    \item \code{dai-duong} is \emph{đái đường} (diabetes), but also \emph{đại dương} (ocean), as in \emph{cá ngừ đại dương} (ocean tuna);
    \item \code{duong} also appears in \emph{dưỡng ẩm} (moisturising) and \emph{dương tính} (positive, of a test result).
\end{itemize}
These pages pass the slug test but have nothing to do with diabetes. So after downloading, every page is checked again on its real text, \textbf{with accents}, where the words can no longer be confused. The check counts how often the page mentions diabetes (\emph{tiểu đường}, \emph{đái tháo đường}, \emph{ĐTĐ}---the ``diabetes count'') and how often it uses related terms (\emph{đường huyết}, \emph{insulin}, \emph{HbA1c}, \emph{biến chứng}, …---the ``related count''). A page is kept if:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item it is an official Vinmec \textbf{disease page}: Vinmec has already classified it as a diabetes page; or
    \item its \textbf{title or summary} mentions diabetes and the text mentions it at least twice: the title is the author's own statement of what the article is about, so it is the strongest signal; or
    \item its slug had a \textbf{strong} keyword and the text mentions diabetes at least three times: this removes the accent collisions, which mention diabetes zero times; or
    \item its slug had only a \textbf{weak} keyword and the text mentions diabetes at least five times \emph{and} uses related terms at least five times: an article ``Insulin là gì?'' that discusses the hormone thirty times but mentions diabetes once is about biology, not the disease.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Parsing a Page}

The crawler does not save the raw HTML of a page. Raw HTML contains menus, advertisements, booking forms, the footer and scripts; the article itself is a small part of it. The parser uses the structure of Vinmec's pages:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{articles}: the body is the element with class \code{entry}; the title, publication date, author and summary come from the page's structured metadata (\code{application/ld+json}, the format search engines read);
    \item \textbf{disease pages}: each section is an element with class \code{item\_detial\_sick} containing a heading and a body.
\end{itemize}
The body is split into \textbf{sections}. For each section three things are saved: the \textbf{heading} it sits under, its \textbf{paragraphs} and its \textbf{bullet points} as separate items.

Keeping the heading is the most important design decision of the whole project. The heading tells us what kind of information the text below it contains: the bullet ``Mệt mỏi'' (fatigue) means something completely different under \emph{Triệu chứng} (symptoms) than under \emph{Tác dụng phụ của thuốc} (side effects of a drug). Section~\ref{sec:extraction} builds the whole extraction on this.

The text at the end of articles that is not part of the article is cut off: the booking text (\emph{``Để đặt lịch khám tại viện, Quý khách vui lòng bấm số HOTLINE…''}), the app advertisement and the ``Xem thêm:'' (see also) links. If it were kept, every article would ``mention'' the same booking words, and the related-article links would bring in words from other topics. \section{Knowledge Graph Design}
\label{sec:kg}

\subsection{Facts, Entities and Relations}

A Knowledge Graph is a set of \textbf{triples}. Each triple states one fact in a fixed three-part form: the thing the fact is about (the \textbf{subject}), the kind of relationship (the \textbf{relation}) and the other thing (the \textbf{object}). The paragraph of the type 2 disease page that lists the classic symptoms and the chronic complications becomes facts such as:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
subject & relation & object \\
\midrule
Đái tháo đường type 2 & HAS\_SYMPTOM & Khát nhiều \\
Đái tháo đường type 2 & HAS\_SYMPTOM & Tiểu nhiều \\
Đái tháo đường type 2 & HAS\_COMPLICATION & Bệnh võng mạc \\
Bệnh võng mạc & AFFECTS\_ORGAN & Mắt \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Why is this better than the paragraph? Because every fact has the same predictable form. A program does not need to understand Vietnamese grammar to use it; it only looks for facts whose subject is ``type 2'' and whose relation is \code{HAS\_COMPLICATION}. The ambiguity of language is resolved once, when the fact is created, instead of every time a question is asked.

If every thing is drawn as a dot and every fact as an arrow, the facts form a network: a \textbf{graph}. The things are the \textbf{nodes} or \textbf{entities}, the facts are the \textbf{edges} or \textbf{relationships}, and each node also has a \textbf{type} (\code{Symptom}, \code{Drug}, …) so a program knows what kind of thing it is. Figure~\ref{fig:toy} shows seven hand-written facts as a graph. Questions become walks along the arrows: ``which organ does type 2 damage?'' starts at \emph{type 2}, follows \code{HAS\_COMPLICATION} to \emph{Bệnh thận đái tháo đường} and then \code{AFFECTS\_ORGAN} to \emph{Thận}. No single fact contains the answer; combining two facts through the node in the middle does. This is what the lecture calls a \textbf{two-hop} query, as opposed to a \textbf{one-hop} query that only reads the arrows directly attached to one node.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{toy_graph.png}
\caption{Seven hand-written diabetes facts drawn as a graph (from the notebook, Part 1). The organ \emph{Thận} is two hops from \emph{type 2}: no single fact connects them.}
\label{fig:toy}
\end{figure}

The real graph has the same shape but is built automatically from 1,096 articles. The hard part of the project is not storing the graph---it is extracting \textbf{correct} facts from free Vietnamese text without a person reading every article. The rest of this section explains how that is done.

The five \code{DiabetesType} nodes are the subjects of almost all extracted facts.

\subsection{Extraction Method}
\label{sec:extraction}

\subsubsection{Why not let each bullet point become a node}

The simplest approach would be to make every bullet under \emph{Triệu chứng} a Symptom node. On the type 2 page that gives nodes such as ``Ăn nhiều'' and ``Uống nhiều (hay khát nước)'', which looks acceptable. But a bullet under \emph{Điều trị} on the type 1 page reads:

\begin{quote}\small
\emph{``Kiểm soát đường huyết: đối với tuýp 1 thì kiểm soát bằng insulin ngoại sinh là chủ yếu. Dùng theo phác đồ, tuân thủ giờ tiêm và liều lượng tránh bị tụt đường huyết. Các loại insulin gồm có…''}
\end{quote}

That is sixty words. As a node it is useless: no other article will ever write the same sixty words, so it never connects to anything and no question ever matches it. Even short bullets differ: one article writes \emph{uống nhiều}, another \emph{khát nước}, another \emph{hay khát}. As separate nodes, the single fact ``diabetes causes thirst'' would be split into weak pieces that never meet.

So instead of copying text into the graph, the project decides \textbf{in advance} which concepts the graph may contain, gives each one a clean name, and searches the text for any of the ways it can be written. Extraction then answers three questions for every section of every page:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Which diabetes is the page about?} This is the subject of the facts.
    \item \textbf{What kind of information may this section contain?} This is decided by the section heading.
    \item \textbf{Which known concepts does the section mention?} These are the objects of the facts.
\end{enumerate}
Each fact found on a page is one \textbf{vote} from that page; the votes of all pages are counted and a fact enters the graph only when enough different pages agree. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the process on a real section.

\subsubsection{The vocabulary and entity normalisation}

The vocabulary (\code{kg/vocab.py}) lists 106 concepts. Each has a clean \textbf{canonical name} that becomes the node, a \textbf{type}, and a list of \textbf{aliases}: the different ways the articles write it (320 aliases in total). A few entries:

\begin{codeblock}
VOCAB = {
    "Khát nhiều":    ("Symptom", ["khát nhiều", "uống nhiều", "khát nước", "hay khát", "khô miệng"]),
    "Tê bì tay chân": ("Symptom", ["tê bì", "tê tay", "tê chân", "dị cảm", "ngứa ran", "kiến bò"]),
    "Kháng insulin": ("RiskFactor", ["kháng insulin", "đề kháng insulin"]),
    "Bệnh võng mạc": ("Complication", ["võng mạc", "biến chứng mắt", "mù lòa"]),
    "HbA1c":         ("Test", ["hba1c", "hemoglobin a1c", "a1c"]),
    "Metformin":     ("Drug", ["metformin", "glucophage"]),
    "Empagliflozin": ("Drug", ["empagliflozin", "jardiance"]),
    "Rau xanh":      ("Food", ["rau xanh", "rau lá xanh", "rau củ", "bông cải", "súp lơ"]),
    ...
}
\end{codeblock}

Mapping many surface forms to one node is called \textbf{entity normalisation}. It is what makes evidence add up: when one article says \emph{uống nhiều} and another \emph{khát nước}, both vote for the same fact \emph{(diabetes, HAS\_SYMPTOM, Khát nhiều)}, and the graph knows that two articles agree. Brand names are included as aliases (\emph{Glucophage}, \emph{Diamicron}, \emph{Januvia}, \emph{Jardiance}, \emph{Lantus}) because Vietnamese articles often name drugs by brand.

The vocabulary was written by hand from the topics that appear in the articles: the classic symptoms, the risk factors listed on Vinmec's pages, the acute and chronic complications, the standard tests, the drugs used in Vietnam, and the foods and lifestyle measures that diet articles discuss. Writing it by hand has an obvious cost---a concept that is not in the list can never be found---and an obvious benefit: every node in the graph is clean, meaningful and correctly typed. The cost is analysed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsubsection{Finding an alias in the text}

All aliases of an entity are combined into one regular expression. For \emph{Tiểu nhiều}:
\begin{codeblock}
(?<!\w)(đi tiểu thường xuyên|tiểu thường xuyên|đi tiểu nhiều|tiểu nhiều|tiểu đêm)(?!\w)
\end{codeblock}
Three details of this pattern matter.

\textbf{The alias must be a whole word.} Vietnamese writes syllables separated by spaces, and many aliases are one short syllable that also appears inside other words. Without protection, the fruit alias \emph{lê} (pear) would match inside \emph{lên} (as in \emph{lên cân}, gaining weight) and the alias \emph{bia} (beer) inside \emph{biên}. The parts \code{(?<!\textbackslash w)} and \code{(?!\textbackslash w)} require that the characters before and after the alias are not letters: ``bệnh nhân lên cân'' contains no food, ``ăn quả lê'' does.

\textbf{Longer aliases are tried first.} If ``tiểu thường xuyên'' were tried before ``đi tiểu thường xuyên'', the shorter alias would match first and the more precise wording would never be used.

\textbf{Accents are kept.} Text is lower-cased so that ``HbA1C'' and ``hba1c'' match, but accents are never removed: removing them would bring back exactly the collisions seen in the URLs (\emph{tiểu đường} and \emph{tiểu dương}).

What word boundaries cannot prevent is an alias that is itself a whole word with a second meaning: the fruit alias \emph{táo} (apple) matches \emph{táo bón} (constipation). The section rule below is what keeps such errors rare, because a food alias only counts inside diet sections.

\subsubsection{The section rule: the heading decides what may be extracted}
\label{sec:section-rule}

Finding a vocabulary word somewhere in an article does not mean that the article states a fact about it. Real examples from the corpus:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{mệt mỏi} (fatigue) appears in a diet article: ``ăn sáng đầy đủ giúp bạn đỡ mệt mỏi''. This is not a statement that fatigue is a symptom of diabetes.
    \item \emph{tăng huyết áp} (high blood pressure) appears in drug paragraphs as a condition the drug should not be combined with.
    \item almost every article mentions \emph{insulin} while explaining how blood sugar works, not because insulin is the treatment being discussed.
\end{itemize}
If every match counted, the graph would claim that diabetes has dozens of ``symptoms'' that are merely words that happened to appear. So a match only counts when the \textbf{section heading} says that this section is about that kind of information. This works because health articles---especially on a hospital website---are organised by exactly these headings. Table~\ref{tab:section-rule} gives the rule.

A section whose heading contains none of these keywords---for example \emph{Tổng quan} (overview)---produces no facts. This is intentional: an overview mentions everything briefly, and the project prefers to miss a fact rather than invent one.

\paragraph{An extra check for symptoms and risk factors.} The first version of the rule had a weakness that the quality check in Section~\ref{sec:precision} revealed. A heading can contain \emph{nguyên nhân} or \emph{nguy cơ} and still not be about diabetes itself:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{``Một số nguyên nhân gây ngứa bàn tay và bàn chân''} lists the causes of \textbf{itching};
    \item \emph{``Yếu tố nguy cơ gây tai biến mạch máu não ở người tiểu đường''} lists the risk factors for a \textbf{stroke} in people who already have diabetes;
    \item \emph{``Làm gì để giảm thiểu nguy cơ tim mạch…''} is advice on \textbf{reducing} heart risk.
\end{itemize}
Reading the bullets under these headings as risk factors \emph{of diabetes} produced wrong facts. The reason is that a symptom or a risk factor always belongs to \emph{something}, and the heading names what that something is. So, for these two types only, the heading must also pass the function \code{about\_disease()}:

\begin{codeblock}
DISEASE_TERMS = ["tiểu đường", "đái tháo đường", "đtđ", "tuýp", "type", "típ", "bệnh"]
OTHER_TOPIC   = ["biến chứng", "thận", "võng mạc", "mắt", "thần kinh", "bàn chân", "tim mạch",
                 "ngứa", "gout", "da", "răng", "nhiễm toan", "ceton", "hạ đường huyết",
                 "thai nhi", "trẻ sinh ra", "tai biến", "đột quỵ", "mạch vành", "hôn mê"]
REDUCE_VERBS  = ["giảm", "phòng", "tránh", "ngừa", "hạn chế", "kiểm soát"]

def about_disease(heading):
    """Generic heading ("Triệu chứng", "Ai dễ bị?") or one that names diabetes, and no other topic."""
    h = re.sub(r"^[\d\.\s]+", "", heading.lower())          # drop numbering such as "2.1."
    if any(t in h for t in OTHER_TOPIC) or any(v + " nguy cơ" in h for v in REDUCE_VERBS):
        return False
    return any(t in h for t in DISEASE_TERMS) or len(h.split()) <= 5

def allowed_labels(heading):
    h = heading.lower()
    labels = set()
    for keys, labs in SECTION_RULES:                          # the rule of Table 7
        if any(k in h for k in keys):
            labels |= labs
    if not about_disease(heading):
        labels -= {"Symptom", "RiskFactor"}
    return labels
\end{codeblock}

\subsubsection{Which diabetes is the subject?}

Diabetes is not one disease. Type 1 is an autoimmune disease in which the body destroys its own insulin-producing cells, usually starting young. Type 2 develops slowly in adults and is linked to weight and lifestyle. Gestational diabetes appears during pregnancy. Prediabetes is the stage before type 2. Their symptoms, causes and treatments overlap but are not identical: ``autoimmune destruction of beta cells'' is a cause of type 1 only, ``pre-eclampsia'' a complication of gestational diabetes only. Attaching every fact to one ``diabetes'' node would lose these differences.

Each page is therefore assigned a subject from its \textbf{title}, because the title is the author's own short statement of what the article is about; counting words in the body is less reliable, since an article about type 2 often mentions type 1 for comparison. The title is checked for the words of each type in turn (\emph{tuýp 1 / type 1 / típ 1}, \emph{tuýp 2 / type 2}, \emph{thai kỳ / mang thai / mẹ bầu}, \emph{tiền đái tháo đường / tiền tiểu đường}); if none is found, the subject is diabetes in general.

This is a simplification with a visible consequence (Figure~\ref{fig:subjects}): most articles do not name a type in the title, so the general node receives most of the facts, even though many of those articles are in fact about type 2. It was accepted deliberately: placing a type 2 fact on the general node makes the graph less specific, while placing it on the type 1 node would make it false.

\subsubsection{Counting evidence: why one page is not enough}

Every rule above can still go wrong on a single page: an unusual heading, a symptom mentioned in passing, a bullet that lists something for comparison. If every page were trusted, every one of these mistakes would become a fact. The defence is to require \textbf{agreement between independent pages}. For every fact the extractor keeps the \emph{set} of pages that voted for it---a set, so a page that repeats ``khát nhiều'' five times counts once. The size of the set is the fact's \textbf{support}.

A small example with three pages about type 2:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Page & Facts it votes for \\
\midrule
A & type 2 HAS\_SYMPTOM Khát nhiều; type 2 HAS\_SYMPTOM Mệt mỏi \\
B & type 2 HAS\_SYMPTOM Khát nhiều \\
C & type 2 HAS\_SYMPTOM Khát nhiều; type 2 HAS\_SYMPTOM Run tay \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\emph{Khát nhiều} is stated by three pages (support 3) and is kept; \emph{Mệt mỏi} and \emph{Run tay} are stated by one page each and are dropped. The idea is the same as checking a claim against a second source. The rule has two exceptions, each with a reason:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a fact from an official Vinmec \textbf{disease page} is accepted with support 1: these are short, structured summaries reviewed by the hospital, with exactly the standard headings, so extraction from them is the most reliable;
    \item a \textbf{side effect} needs support 3, because that extraction works on single sentences and is noisier (below).
\end{itemize}

\begin{codeblock}
for (h, rel, t), urls in support.items():                 # urls = set of pages voting for the fact
    need = MIN_SUPPORT_SIDE_EFFECT if rel == "HAS_SIDE_EFFECT" else MIN_SUPPORT   # 3 or 2
    if len(urls) >= need or (h, rel, t) in from_disease:
        triples.append((h, rel, t, len(urls), "vinmec", " ".join(sorted(urls)[:5])))
\end{codeblock}

The support number is not thrown away after this filter. It is stored on the fact in Neo4j, so queries can rank facts by how well documented they are, and the website lets the reader hide facts below a chosen level of evidence.

\subsubsection{Foods: recommended or to avoid}

For foods the graph has two relations, \code{RECOMMENDS\_FOOD} and \code{AVOIDS\_FOOD}. The first version decided between them from the words in the sentence or heading: \emph{nên ăn} (should eat) meant recommended; \emph{kiêng}, \emph{tránh}, \emph{hạn chế}, \emph{không nên} meant avoid. It failed, and the reason is instructive: diet articles mix both kinds of advice in the same place. A heading such as \emph{``Thực phẩm mẹ bầu cần tránh và nên ăn''} contains both, and a single sentence often says ``hạn chế đồ ngọt, thay vào đó nên ăn rau xanh''. The output contained nonsense such as \emph{gestational diabetes AVOIDS green vegetables}.

The final rule treats whether a food is good or bad for diabetes as a \textbf{property of the food}, written once in the vocabulary: sweets, soft drinks, white rice and refined starch, very sweet fruit (durian, ripe mango, longan, jackfruit), alcohol, fried food and processed meat are marked ``avoid''; the other foods are ``recommended''. The text then only tells us that the food is discussed in a diet section for that type of diabetes. This matches standard nutrition advice and what the articles say in almost all cases; the cost is that nuanced advice (``fruit is fine in moderate amounts'') cannot be represented.

\subsubsection{Drug side effects}

Side effects are scattered across drug sections, complication sections and general articles, so the section rule cannot find them. They are extracted \textbf{inside one sentence} instead: a sentence produces a side-effect fact only if it contains a drug, a symptom or complication, and a word that signals cause---\emph{tác dụng phụ} (side effect), \emph{tác dụng không mong muốn} (unwanted effect) or \emph{gây} (causes):

\begin{quote}\small
``Insulin có thể \textbf{gây hạ đường huyết} nếu tiêm quá liều'' $\Rightarrow$ (Insulin, HAS\_SIDE\_EFFECT, Hạ đường huyết)
\end{quote}

Sentences containing \emph{thiếu} (lack of) or \emph{không đủ} (not enough) are skipped. The sentence ``\emph{thiếu} insulin gây nhiễm toan ceton'' has a drug, a complication and the word \emph{gây}, but it says that a \emph{lack} of insulin causes ketoacidosis---the opposite of a side effect of taking insulin. Without the exception the graph would warn that insulin causes the very condition it treats. Section~\ref{sec:errors} shows that this filter catches most but not all such sentences.

\subsubsection{Background facts added by hand}
\label{sec:curated}

Some knowledge is not something articles state under a heading, but general medical classification that articles take for granted: Metformin belongs to the biguanide class; diabetic retinopathy affects the eye; the diagnostic threshold of HbA1c is 6.5\%. These facts are added by hand and labelled \code{source = curated}, so they can always be told apart from extracted facts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item each drug and its \textbf{drug class} (22 facts: Metformin $\rightarrow$ Biguanide, Empagliflozin $\rightarrow$ Ức chế SGLT2, …);
    \item each complication and the \textbf{organ} it affects (12 facts);
    \item each type of diabetes as a \textbf{subtype} of diabetes in general (4 facts);
    \item the diagnostic \textbf{thresholds} stored as a property of four test nodes, from the Vietnamese Ministry of Health 2020 guideline and the American Diabetes Association criteria that Vinmec's pages also quote: HbA1c $\geq$ 6.5\%; fasting plasma glucose $\geq$ 7.0~mmol/L; 2-hour glucose in the tolerance test $\geq$ 11.1~mmol/L; random glucose $\geq$ 11.1~mmol/L with symptoms.
\end{itemize}
These few facts make the most interesting questions possible. Without ``retinopathy affects the eye'', the graph could say that type 2 causes retinopathy but could not answer ``which organs does type 2 damage?''.

\subsection{Storing the Graph in Neo4j}
\label{sec:neo4j}

\subsubsection{Why a graph database}

The NetworkX graph of the lecture's first examples lives in the memory of a Python program: when the program ends it is gone, and every program that needs it must rebuild it. A real system needs the knowledge stored permanently, shared by several programs (the notebook, the website, Cypher queries) and answered quickly.

One could store the triples in an ordinary relational database as a table with three columns. But questions about a graph are questions about \emph{paths}: start here, follow these arrows, then those. In a table database every step of a path is a join of the table with itself; the queries become long and hard to read and get slower as the table grows. A \textbf{graph database} stores each node with direct references to its neighbours, so following an arrow is a direct jump rather than a search. \textbf{Neo4j} is the most widely used graph database, and its query language \textbf{Cypher} describes paths with an ASCII-art syntax that looks like a drawing of the path. Neo4j 5 runs locally in a Docker container, with its data stored in the project folder.

\subsubsection{Data model}

\begin{codeblock}
(:Entity:Symptom {name})                       every entity: label Entity + its own type
(:Entity:Test {name, threshold})
(:DiabetesType)-[:HAS_SYMPTOM {support, source, example_urls}]->(:Symptom)
(:Article {url, title, page_type, date})-[:ABOUT]->(:DiabetesType)
(:Article)-[:MENTIONS]->(:Entity)
\end{codeblock}

Three decisions in this model deserve an explanation.

\textbf{Every entity has two labels.} The type label (\code{Symptom}) lets a query ask for ``all symptoms''. The shared \code{Entity} label lets one rule cover all entities---every entity name must be unique, so there can never be two different ``Metformin'' nodes---and lets a query such as ``everything connected to Metformin'' be written without knowing in advance the type of each neighbour.

\textbf{The evidence is stored on the relationships.} \code{support}, \code{source} and \code{example\_urls} are properties of each fact. One query can therefore return the answer, how well documented it is and which articles state it. If the evidence were stored elsewhere, every answer would need a second lookup.

\textbf{The articles are nodes too.} Each of the 1,096 articles is an \code{Article} node, connected by \code{ABOUT} to its diabetes type and by \code{MENTIONS} to every entity it mentions anywhere (10,083 links). This keeps the connection between the clean structured facts and the original text, and answers ``where does this come from?''. Because article nodes and \code{MENTIONS} links are not facts about diabetes, all queries about the knowledge itself restrict themselves to \code{Entity} nodes.

\subsubsection{Loading}

\begin{codeblock}
CREATE CONSTRAINT entity_name IF NOT EXISTS FOR (e:Entity) REQUIRE e.name IS UNIQUE;

UNWIND $rows AS r
MERGE (e:Entity {name: r.name}) SET e:Symptom;          -- one query per entity type

UNWIND $rows AS r
MATCH (h:Entity {name: r.subject}), (t:Entity {name: r.object})
MERGE (h)-[x:HAS_SYMPTOM]->(t)                           -- one query per relation type
SET x.support = r.support, x.source = r.source, x.example_urls = r.example_urls;
\end{codeblock}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\code{MERGE} instead of \code{CREATE}.} \code{CREATE} always adds a new node or relationship, even when an identical one exists. \code{MERGE} first looks for it and creates it only if it is missing. The loader can therefore be run any number of times without duplicating anything.
    \item \textbf{\code{UNWIND} sends a whole batch.} Each request to the database has a fixed cost. Sending the 10,083 mention links one by one would mean 10,083 round trips; \code{UNWIND} sends a list of rows in one request and lets the database loop over it. The whole graph loads in a few seconds.
    \item \textbf{The uniqueness constraint is also an index.} To enforce unique names, Neo4j builds an index on \code{name}, so every query that starts from ``the node called Metformin'' jumps directly to it instead of checking all nodes.
    \item \textbf{Relation types are written into the query text.} Cypher does not allow the type of a relationship (or a label) to be a parameter, so the loader groups the facts by relation and builds one query per type from the fixed list of twelve.
\end{itemize}

After loading, the database contains 1,223 nodes (127 entities and 1,096 articles) and 11,518 relationships (339 facts, 10,083 mentions and 1,096 \code{ABOUT} links).

\subsection{Querying with Cypher}

A Cypher query describes the \emph{shape} of a path: round brackets for nodes, square brackets inside an arrow for relationships.

\begin{codeblock}
MATCH (t:DiabetesType {name: 'Đái tháo đường type 2'})-[r:HAS_SYMPTOM]->(s:Symptom)
RETURN s.name, r.support
\end{codeblock}

Read from left to right: find a \code{DiabetesType} node called ``type 2'', follow an outgoing \code{HAS\_SYMPTOM} arrow to a \code{Symptom} node, return the symptom's name and the arrow's support. Neo4j uses the name index to jump to the ``type 2'' node and then looks only at the arrows attached to it; it never scans the rest of the database. The work grows with the number of arrows followed, not with the size of the database. The fifteen example queries of \code{kg/queries.cypher} and their results are presented in Section~\ref{sec:queries}.

\subsection{Graph Retrieval}
\label{sec:retrieval}

In a complete question-answering system (the lecture's Knowledge Graph + RAG architecture), the graph's job is to hand a language model a small set of relevant, reliable facts. \code{kg/retriever.py} implements the lecture's \code{graph\_facts} on Neo4j:

\begin{codeblock}
ONE_HOP = """
MATCH (e:Entity {name: $name})-[r]-(n:Entity)
RETURN startNode(r).name AS s, type(r) AS rel, endNode(r).name AS o, r.support AS support
ORDER BY support DESC"""
TWO_HOP = """
MATCH (e:Entity {name: $name})-[r1]-(m:Entity)-[r2]-(n:Entity) WHERE n <> e
RETURN startNode(r2).name AS s, type(r2) AS rel, endNode(r2).name AS o, r2.support AS support
ORDER BY support DESC"""

def graph_facts(driver, entity, hops=1, limit=30):
    facts, seen = [], set()
    with driver.session() as s:
        for q in [ONE_HOP] + ([TWO_HOP] if hops >= 2 else []):
            for r in s.run(q, name=entity):
                key = (r["s"], r["rel"], r["o"])
                if key not in seen:
                    seen.add(key)
                    facts.append(f"{r['s']} {r['rel']} {r['o']}.")
    return facts[:limit]
\end{codeblock}

It starts at the entity named in the question, collects the facts within one (or two) steps, turns each back into a short sentence, sorts by support and returns at most 30.

\begin{codeblock}
$ python kg/retriever.py "Hạ đường huyết"
Đái tháo đường HAS_COMPLICATION Hạ đường huyết.
Insulin HAS_SIDE_EFFECT Hạ đường huyết.
Đái tháo đường thai kỳ HAS_COMPLICATION Hạ đường huyết.
Metformin HAS_SIDE_EFFECT Hạ đường huyết.
Đái tháo đường type 2 HAS_COMPLICATION Hạ đường huyết.
\end{codeblock}

Why limit the number of hops and facts? In a graph almost everything is connected to everything within a few steps, especially through hub nodes. One step from \emph{Hạ đường huyết} (hypoglycaemia) gives the 5 precise facts above; two steps give \textbf{230}, because one first-step neighbour is the general diabetes node, which is connected to almost the whole graph. Giving 230 facts to a language model for a question about hypoglycaemia would bury the relevant ones in noise. This is the problem the lecture calls ``graph retrieval too broad'' (§23.4), and the solution used here is the one it recommends: start from the entity in the question, stay within one or two hops, and rank and cut the list.

\subsection{The Website}

\subsubsection{Architecture}

Cypher is powerful but not something a typical reader can use. The website makes the graph usable by anyone. It has two parts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item an \textbf{API} (\code{api/}, Python, FastAPI) that receives simple HTTP requests, runs fixed Cypher queries against Neo4j and returns JSON;
    \item a \textbf{web client} (\code{web/}, React 18 and Vite, hand-written CSS, the same stack as the course's earlier web applications) that the user sees in the browser.
\end{itemize}

Stability: the website depends on a small documented interface, not on the database schema, so the schema can change without breaking it. Simplicity: every request is a plain URL such as \code{/entities/Metformin}, which can be tested in a browser and is documented automatically by FastAPI at \code{/docs}.

Every query is parameterised: user input is passed as a parameter (\code{\$name}), never pasted into the query text, so a search for an unusual string cannot change the query. Every response has a declared shape (a Pydantic model), which both validates the output and documents it. Errors are translated into plain messages: an unknown entity gives \code{404 No entity named …}, a stopped database gives \code{503 The Neo4j database is not reachable}, which the website shows as a banner telling the user what to start. The entity endpoint, for example:

\begin{codeblock}
@app.get("/entities/{name}", response_model=EntityDetail)
def entity(name: str, min_support: int = Query(0, ge=0)):
    rows = db.read(f"""
        MATCH (e:Entity {{name: $name}})
        RETURN e.name AS name, {TYPE.format(v='e')} AS type, e.threshold AS threshold,
               COUNT {{ (:Article)-[:MENTIONS|ABOUT]->(e) }} AS mentioned_in""", name=name)
    if not rows:
        raise HTTPException(404, f"No entity named '{name}'.")
    facts = db.read(f"""
        MATCH (e:Entity {{name: $name}})-[r]-(n:Entity)
        WHERE r.source = 'curated' OR r.support >= $min_support
        WITH startNode(r) AS s, r, endNode(r) AS o
        RETURN s.name AS subject, {TYPE.format(v='s')} AS subject_type, type(r) AS relation,
               o.name AS object, {TYPE.format(v='o')} AS object_type,
               coalesce(r.support, 0) AS support, r.source AS source
        ORDER BY support DESC LIMIT $cap""", name=name, min_support=min_support, cap=config.MAX_FACTS)
    return {**rows[0], "facts": facts}
\end{codeblock}

The path finder uses Neo4j's built-in \code{allShortestPaths} over entity nodes only (articles are excluded, otherwise every pair of entities would be connected through some article that mentions both), limited to four steps. For each step it also returns whether the fact points forward or backward along the path, so the website can phrase it correctly (``\emph{is a risk factor for}'' rather than ``\emph{has the risk factor}'').

\subsubsection{Web client}

The client has five pages, each with its own address so that any view can be bookmarked or shared (for example \code{\#/entity/Metformin} or \code{\#/paths?from=…\&to=…}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Home}: a search box with suggestions as one types, the size of the graph, entry points for each diabetes type, and a short ``how this graph was built'' section;
    \item \textbf{Entity}: all facts about one entity, grouped under plain-language headings, each with its support and a \emph{Sources} button that lists the articles stating it; a drawing of the entity's neighbourhood; the latest articles that mention it;
    \item \textbf{Diabetes types}: the same page for each type, in tabs, to compare them;
    \item \textbf{Symptom checker}: choose symptoms and see which types of diabetes the articles associate with them;
    \item \textbf{Connections}: choose two entities and see the shortest chains of facts between them.
\end{enumerate}

Several design decisions follow from the evaluation of the graph rather than from appearance:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Evidence is always visible.} Each fact shows how many articles state it, and its sources are one click away. Hand-added facts are marked ``reference''.
    \item \textbf{The reader chooses the level of evidence.} A selector hides facts stated by fewer than 2, 3, 5 or 10 articles (default 2). This exposes the precision/recall trade-off of Section~\ref{sec:tradeoff} to the user instead of fixing it for them.
    \item \textbf{Relations are written in plain words.} \code{HAS\_COMPLICATION} seen from the disease is shown as ``Complications'', seen from the complication as ``Complication of''; \code{TREATED\_BY} seen from a drug becomes ``Used to treat''.
    \item \textbf{The medical disclaimer is explicit} where it matters, on the symptom checker: the result shows what the articles say and is not a diagnosis.
    \item \textbf{Every state is handled}: loading indicators, empty results, an unknown entity, and a stopped API or database (a banner with the command to start it). The search box is a standard accessible combobox (arrow keys, Enter, Escape), and the layout adapts to phone screens.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Which Entities Are the Most Important?}
\label{sec:pagerank}

``Important'' can mean different things for a node in a graph, and two common measures give very different answers here. Understanding why is a good way to understand the graph itself.

\textbf{Degree} is the number of facts an entity takes part in. It answers ``how many facts involve this entity?'' and looks only at the node's own arrows.

\textbf{PageRank}, the method Google originally used to rank web pages, says that a node is important if \emph{important} nodes point to it. It is easiest to understand as a walk: a person starts on a random node and, at each step, either follows one of the outgoing arrows of the current node at random or, occasionally (15\% of the time), jumps to a completely random node. After a very long walk, a node's PageRank is the share of time spent on it. Nodes that many paths lead into are visited often.

\textbf{What happens in this graph.} All arrows point away from the diabetes types: type $\rightarrow$ drug $\rightarrow$ drug class, type $\rightarrow$ complication $\rightarrow$ organ. A walker following the arrows drifts down these chains and arrives at their ends---drug classes and organs---which have no outgoing arrows, so the walker stays there until the next random jump. Directed PageRank therefore ranks \emph{Nhóm Insulin}, \emph{Ức chế DPP-4} and \emph{Mắt} (eye) highest. They are not what the articles are mainly about; they are simply where many paths end.

The direction of the arrows is our own modelling choice (``type 2 HAS\_SYMPTOM thirst'' could be written ``thirst SYMPTOM\_OF type 2''), and a measure whose answer flips when a relation is renamed does not measure what we want. PageRank is therefore also computed \textbf{ignoring direction}, as if every arrow could be walked both ways. Then an entity is important if it is well connected to other well-connected entities. This ranking makes sense: the diabetes types first, then insulin, cardiovascular disease, stroke, diabetic kidney disease, retinopathy and Metformin---the treatments and complications that tie the whole graph together (Figure~\ref{fig:pagerank}). The lesson is that every graph measure answers a specific question, and one must check that it is the question one wants to ask.

\subsection{Correctness of the Facts}
\label{sec:precision}

\subsubsection{What was measured and how}

The measure is \textbf{precision}: of the facts the graph contains, what share are correct. Precision was chosen because it is what a user of the graph experiences---every wrong fact is something the system will state with confidence. The procedure (\code{kg/evaluate.py}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item draw 50 of the extracted facts at random, with a fixed random seed (42) so that the same sample can be drawn again;
    \item for each fact, show the article title and an evidence sentence from a section that the extractor actually used;
    \item judge the fact by reading \textbf{all} its supporting articles, not only the sentence shown, and record \code{correct} and an \code{error\_type} in \code{evaluation/manual\_audit.csv}.
\end{enumerate}

A fact counts as correct when the Vinmec articles state it for that type of diabetes. This is a check against the source, not against clinical guidelines; a medical review would be a different and much more expensive evaluation. Why random facts and why fifty? Random, because choosing the facts by hand would unconsciously favour easy ones and make the result look better than it is. Fifty, because each fact requires reading several articles; fifty is enough to estimate precision with useful accuracy and to reveal the main kinds of error.

\section{Error Analysis}
\label{sec:errors}

The quality check of Section~\ref{sec:precision} found two wrong facts out of fifty. This section explains why they were wrong, what was changed, what the change cost, and which other errors are known to remain.

\subsection{The Two Errors in the Sample}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small

Both errors have the same cause: a fact that belongs to a \textbf{complication} was attached to the disease itself. The section rule allowed it because the headings contained an allowed keyword (\emph{nguy cơ}, \emph{nguyên nhân}) or because complication sections were, in the first version, also allowed to produce symptoms. The rule looked at \emph{what kind} of information a heading announces, but not \emph{whose} symptoms or risk factors they are.

Two observations made the diagnosis clear. First, both wrong facts had high support (16 and 18), so the support filter could not catch them: many articles discuss the complications of diabetes, and each one contributed a wrong vote. A filter based on agreement only removes errors that are random; it cannot remove an error that the rules make systematically. Second, both errors were in the two relation types (symptoms and risk factors) where the owner of the information matters---a complication always belongs to diabetes, but a symptom may belong to diabetes or to one of its complications.

\subsection{The Fix}

The fix is the \code{about\_disease()} check of Section~\ref{sec:section-rule}: for symptoms and risk factors, the heading must name diabetes or be short and generic, must not name another topic (a complication, an organ, itching, stroke, coma, …), and must not be about reducing a risk; and complication sections no longer produce symptoms. Re-running the extraction changed the graph from 345 to 339 facts.

\end{table}

Two of the six removals were real errors that the sample had not happened to contain. The other four were true facts. Each had exactly one good supporting article and one weak one (for example, the age and blood-pressure facts for prediabetes came partly from the article ``Chăm sóc các vấn đề tim mạch ở người bệnh tiểu đường và tiền tiểu đường'', whose risk-factor bullets sit under a heading about heart risk). The stricter rule removed the weak vote, the support fell to 1, and the minimum of two articles removed the fact. One consequence is visible in Table~\ref{tab:entity-types}: the risk factor \emph{Dùng corticoid kéo dài} lost its only fact and no longer appears in the graph.

And the two original errors? They stayed in the graph, but with far less support: 16 $\rightarrow$ 3 articles for high cholesterol and 18 $\rightarrow$ 6 for abdominal pain. Reading the remaining articles showed that they \emph{do} state these facts under genuine diabetes headings: ``Nguyên nhân và yếu tố nguy cơ gây bệnh tiểu đường: … Rối loạn mỡ máu'', and abdominal pain in symptom lists for diabetes in children (where it is the sign of ketoacidosis at onset). By the definition used here---the articles state it---they are now properly supported. The problem had been the weak supporting articles, not the facts.

This is the \textbf{precision/recall trade-off} that every extraction system faces. Strict rules produce fewer wrong facts but miss more true ones; loose rules find more true facts but let in more wrong ones. No setting is best for every use. This is why the support count is kept on every fact instead of being used only once as a filter: a careful application can require three or five articles, while someone exploring the data can see everything. The website exposes exactly this choice to its users.

\subsection{Other Known Errors}

The sample of fifty facts cannot find every error. Inspecting the queries and the graph revealed four more.

\paragraph{A wrong side effect.} The graph contains \emph{Insulin HAS\_SIDE\_EFFECT Nhiễm toan ceton} (ketoacidosis), supported by five articles. It is wrong: ketoacidosis is caused by a \emph{lack} of insulin and is \emph{treated} with insulin. The filter of Section~\ref{sec:extraction} skips sentences with \emph{thiếu} or \emph{không đủ}, but the articles connect insulin and ketoacidosis in other ways that the rule ``drug + complication + \emph{gây} in one sentence'' cannot tell apart:
\begin{itemize}
    \item insulin as the \textbf{treatment}: ``nhiễm toan ceton … có thể \emph{gây} nguy hiểm đến tính mạng nếu như không được xử trí kịp thời bằng insulin'';
    \item another drug causing it \textbf{together with} insulin: ``Danazol: dùng cùng với Insulin do có thể \emph{gây} tăng glucose máu và nhiễm toan ceton'';
    \item hormones that \textbf{work against} insulin: ``Những hormone này … có tác dụng ngược lại với insulin … sẽ \emph{gây} ra toan ceton''.
\end{itemize}
In each sentence the word \emph{gây} belongs to something other than insulin. Deciding \emph{what} causes \emph{what} requires understanding the grammar of the sentence, which cue words cannot do. Sentence-level relation extraction is the weakest part of the method; Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} proposes a language model for it.

\paragraph{An alias with a second meaning.} The fruit alias \emph{táo} (apple) also matches \emph{táo bón} (constipation). Word boundaries cannot prevent this because \emph{táo} is a whole word in both. The section rule limits the damage---a food only counts in diet sections---but a diet section that mentions constipation would produce a false vote for ``low-sugar fruit''.

\paragraph{A concept hidden by a longer one.} The vocabulary contains \emph{Hạ đường huyết sơ sinh} (hypoglycaemia of the newborn) separately from \emph{Hạ đường huyết} (hypoglycaemia). Articles rarely use the full phrase; they say ``trẻ sinh ra có thể bị hạ đường huyết'', which matches the general entity. The newborn-specific entity therefore never reached two articles and is absent from the graph, while its facts are counted under the general hypoglycaemia (which is why hypoglycaemia is the top complication of gestational diabetes).

\paragraph{Dominance of the general node.} Because the subject comes from the title only, the general diabetes node collects most facts (Figure~\ref{fig:subjects}). This is not an error in a single fact, but it makes type-specific questions less complete than they could be.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The lecture lists four ways in which a Knowledge Graph can fail: missing entities, incorrect relationships, duplicate entities and outdated information. Each applies to this project in a specific way, and a few further limitations come from the design choices.

\paragraph{Missing entities (unknown recall).} The vocabulary is fixed: 106 concepts written in 320 ways. Anything not in it can never be found, however often the articles discuss it---a newer drug or insulin brand, a rarer complication, a traditional remedy, a specific test. The evaluation measured how many extracted facts are correct (precision) but not how many true facts in the articles were missed (recall). Measuring recall would require annotating a set of articles by hand with every fact they contain, which was outside the scope of this project.

\paragraph{Incorrect relationships.} About 4\% of the sampled facts were wrong (likely range 1--13\%), mostly facts about a complication attached to the disease. Section headings are a strong signal of meaning but not a perfect one, and the side-effect extraction from cue words in single sentences is weaker still (Section~\ref{sec:errors}).

\paragraph{Duplicates and granularity.} Aliases deliberately merge near-synonyms---\emph{tê bì}, \emph{dị cảm} and \emph{kiến bò} all become \emph{Tê bì tay chân}---and sometimes merge things a doctor would keep apart, such as hypoglycaemia of the mother and of the newborn. Conversely, a concept written in a way the vocabulary does not know is simply missed rather than duplicated, so true duplicates are rare.

\paragraph{Outdated information.} All articles carry a 2024 publication date that reflects Vinmec's re-publication of its archive, not when each article was written, and an old article counts the same as a new one. Medical advice changes (new drug classes, revised targets), and the graph has no notion of time. A real system would re-crawl regularly and record the date of each fact.

\paragraph{Coarse subjects.} The type of diabetes is taken only from the article title, so many facts that are really about type 2 are attached to the general diabetes node.

\paragraph{Fixed food advice.} Whether a food is recommended or to be avoided is decided by the food, not read from the article, so conditional advice (``in moderate amounts'', ``choose low-sugar varieties'') cannot be represented.

\paragraph{One source.} All facts come from one hospital's website. This makes the graph consistent, but it inherits that source's emphases and gaps, and ``correct'' in this report means ``stated by Vinmec'', not ``checked against current clinical guidelines''.

\paragraph{Hand-added facts.} Drug classes, affected organs and diagnostic thresholds were added by hand. They are clearly labelled \code{curated} and are standard medical knowledge, but they were not learned from the articles.

\paragraph{Not medical advice.} The graph summarises public health articles. The symptom checker shows which types of diabetes the articles associate with a set of symptoms; it does not diagnose anyone. The website states this where it matters.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{What Was Achieved}

This project built a Knowledge Graph of diabetes knowledge from Vietnamese health articles, stored it in Neo4j and made it explorable through a website.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data (R1).} 1,197 candidate pages were found through Vinmec's sitemap; a relevance filter that works on the accented text removed accent collisions and off-topic pages, leaving 1,096 diabetes articles (1.13 million words), saved section by section.
    \item \textbf{Knowledge Graph (R2, R3).} An extractor that combines a hand-written vocabulary, the section headings of the articles and agreement between articles produced 127 entities of 10 types and 339 facts of 12 relation types, stored as \code{subject, relation, object} CSV with support and sources---well above the lecture's minimums of 50 entities, 100 relationships, 5 relation types and 3 entity types.
    \item \textbf{Neo4j and queries (R4, R6).} The graph is stored in Neo4j with its evidence on every relationship and its source articles as nodes. Fifteen Cypher queries, including one-hop, two-hop, aggregation, path and provenance queries, answer the questions the system was designed for; \code{graph\_facts} retrieves the facts around an entity within one or two hops, and the evaluation showed why this bound is necessary (5 facts at one hop, 230 at two).
    \item \textbf{Visualisation (R5).} The graph was shown as a whole, by neighbourhood, by drug family and by diabetes type, and analysed with degree, directed and undirected PageRank, and article mentions.
    \item \textbf{Website (R7).} A small API and a React client let anyone search the graph, read the facts about an entity with their sources, compare diabetes types, check symptoms and find the chains of facts that connect two entities.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation (R8, R9).} 48 of 50 randomly sampled facts were correct (0.96; likely range 0.87--0.99). The two errors shared one cause, which was fixed; the fix's cost in lost true facts was measured and explained as a precision/recall trade-off.
\end{itemize}

The main lesson of the project is that \textbf{structure already present in the text can do most of the work}. Health articles are organised under standard headings, and using those headings to decide what kind of fact a sentence can contain, together with requiring independent articles to agree, produced a reliable graph without any trained model. The quality check also showed the limit of agreement: it removes random noise, but not an error the rules make systematically---only looking at the errors revealed that.

\subsection{Future Work}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Measure recall.} Annotate a sample of articles by hand with every fact they state, and measure how many the extractor finds. This would quantify the main unknown of the project.
    \item \textbf{Grow the vocabulary semi-automatically.} Frequent noun phrases that appear under symptom or treatment headings but match no vocabulary entry are candidates for new entities; a person would only need to approve them.
    \item \textbf{Better relation extraction.} Use a language model to extract relations sentence by sentence (``does this sentence say that X causes Y, and is X taken or lacking?''), checked against the section rule. This would address the side-effect errors and allow new relations such as \emph{food prevents complication}.
    \item \textbf{Knowledge Graph + RAG.} Connect the graph to the retrieval-augmented chat system of Assignment 08: the graph supplies the facts and their paths, the article chunks supply detailed explanations, and the answer cites both.
    \item \textbf{Freshness.} Re-crawl on a schedule, store the date of each article on its facts, and prefer recent evidence when articles disagree.
\end{enumerate}
